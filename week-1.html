WEEK 1

Topic, challenges and current data manifestations

Mozart’s piano music: A Music Curation and Analytics portfolio

Topic

The topic I have chosen for my Music Curation and Analytics project is the composer Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. I chose Mozart because I played many of his piano works during my childhood. As a result, I am familiar with his musical style and piano compositions, and I have long admired the elegance and expressiveness of his music. Although Mozart’s body of work is extremely wide-ranging, including symphonies and operas, in this project I will focus primarily on his piano sonatas and related piano works. For example, in the subsequent MuseScore Studio task, I plan to use the third movement, Rondo alla Turca, from Piano Sonata No. 11 in A major, K. 331.

Challenges and current data manifestations

Challenges

  1. Multiple versions. The same musical work can exist in many different versions. A piece may have an original manuscript, several printed editions, modern digital scores and many audio recordings. In Mozart’s case, the same piano sonata may be performed by many different pianists, and each performance can differ in tempo, dynamics and interpretation. Therefore, it is difficult to decide which version should be treated as the main or representative version in a dataset.
  2. Fragmented resources. Music data is often distributed across different platforms. Scores, metadata and recordings are not always stored in the same archive. For Mozart, for example, the Digital Mozart Edition provides detailed information and digital scores, while IMSLP may provide other score editions and some recordings. This means that it takes a great deal of time to collect data from different platforms.
  3. Copyright and access. Mozart’s compositions themselves are in the public domain, but some modern editions and recent recordings may still be protected by copyright. Therefore, materials may be visible online but not always freely reusable.

Current data manifestations

A substantial amount of well-curated digital data is already available on Mozart’s music. Resources such as the Digital Mozart Edition collect and organise digital scores together with metadata such as titles, Köchel catalogue numbers and genre. IMSLP provides access to scanned score editions, while MuseScore.com hosts scores shared by its community. In addition, video recordings of Mozart’s works are widely available on YouTube.

These resources usually present music through searchable databases. Users can search by work title, catalogue number or sometimes performer. Scores are commonly presented as scanned pages or digital notation, while metadata is shown as structured descriptive information. Video recordings are generally presented as individual performance videos, with descriptive information of varying detail.

As a result, Mozart’s music is highly accessible and relatively well organised. Digital archives also make it easier than before for users to access and compare musical materials across platforms. However, there are also weaknesses. The links between scores, metadata and recordings are not always clearly established. Furthermore, the large number of editions and recordings can make it difficult for users to choose suitable materials. For those compiling datasets, it remains necessary to establish a relatively uniform set of selection criteria and use stable identifiers such as Köchel numbers to link different types of data.
